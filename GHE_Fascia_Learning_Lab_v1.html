An honest note on "release"

The felt melting sensation during a hold may be mostly muscle relaxation and nervous-system change, not the fascia itself lengthening (Purslow's caution). The experience is real and useful; the explanation is still debated. Emerging

How long does change take?

Three ranges appear in the library and they measure different things: roughly 3 to 9 months for noticeable change in one source, 6 to 24 months in Schleip's "Bamboo Gardener" image, and 12 to 24 months for deeper remodeling of habitual patterns in Avison. Sensation can change in minutes, habits in weeks, structure slowly. Emerging

Remodeling clock

Collagen renews slowly. Choose how often you practice and see a realistic window. Established for the principle; exact timing varies.

5. When a student floods or checks out

  1. Pause the room. Say plainly: 'Let us all take a pause. Stay where you are or sit up, your choice.' Do not single anyone out.
  2. Slow your own voice. Lower and slow your tone. Name things in the room: the wall, the floor, the window.
  3. Offer the Dial. 'You can soften, steady, wake up or slow down. Any of them is right.' Nothing more.
  4. Offer orienting, not eye contact. Invite the whole room to look around and find something blue or something round. Never ask for eye contact.
  5. Offer a body anchor. Feet pressed into the floor, hands pressing together, back against the wall. Student chooses; you do not demonstrate touch.
  6. Offer the Comfort Corner. 'The corner is open. You can go, stay, or come back in a few minutes.' Do not escort, do not hover.
  7. Keep it simple and brief. One or two short sentences. No questions about what happened. No asking for a story.
  8. Repair and follow up later. After class, send a short note: what you saw, what you changed, that nothing is required. If the student returns, begin with choice, not with the event.

Scope, Referral and Crisis Resources

This course is education and practice, not therapy, medical care or diagnosis. It teaches self-regulation and gentle movement. Anything that looks like treatment belongs to a licensed professional.

When to refer

  • Student reports abuse, assault, self-harm or thoughts of suicide
  • Student describes flashbacks, dissociation, panic or numbness that keep coming back
  • Pain, numbness, weakness or swelling that is new, worsening or unexplained
  • Pregnancy, recent surgery, osteoporosis, pacemaker, anticoagulants, cancer history, lymphedema or neuropathy
  • Student asks you to interpret what their body 'means' or to work on trauma content
  • You feel out of your depth, or you are carrying too much

The Goldilocks lens

Segarra says the just-right version of a pose differs from body to body. She uses the Ayurvedic doshas (Vata, Pitta, Kapha) as her vocabulary for that. The course keeps the principle: change the pose to fit the body, never the body to fit a picture. The doshas are an optional lens from your own traditions, not a test. Interpretive

6 · Nervous system and safety

Aim to work inside the green zone. When you drift out, orient and use a resource, then return in small steps.

Safety comes before depth. Choose a tool to see how to use it.

Brake before accelerator

Rothschild teaches that you learn to brake before you learn to accelerate: do not speed up until you can slow or stop reliably. Check in often, watch breath, color and pace, and stop when signs of high arousal rise. Established

Quick, present-tense body check-ins anchor. Lingering a long time on one sensation can stir memory and is not right for someone close to overwhelm.

Dual awareness

Hold two things at once: an inner sensation and the outer room. "Notice the tightness in your chest, and also notice the colors you can see right now." Switch back and forth four times, and rate steadiness 0 to 10 before and after.

Before you teach: cautions checklist

  • Acute infection, fever or inflammation: no pressure.
  • Open wounds, fresh bruising, burns or skin conditions in the area.
  • Suspected blood clot: no pressure or rolling on the legs; refer.
  • Recent fracture, surgery or injury: wait for clearance.
  • Blood thinners, severe osteoporosis or cancer: medical clearance first.
  • Numbness or neuropathy: very light work only.
  • Scars: self-directed, light, only when fully healed.
  • Pregnancy: avoid deep work; refer to a prenatal-trained practitioner.

Stop and refer for sharp pain, new numbness, chest pain, shortness of breath, dizziness, or an emotional surge that does not settle. Teach movement and self-care, not diagnosis or trauma treatment.

Contrast case: pressure

One book in the library advises firmer pressure and calls a technique "very uncomfortable." Another says pressure should match tolerance and movement should be "never painful." A trauma-aware teacher follows the second: discomfort can be a threat signal, and a bracing body does not release.

Prune the claim: emotion and fascia

Tears or memories sometimes show up after a release. The experience is real. The claim that fascia stores specific emotions is anecdotal. Interpretive Honor what you feel, and never interpret it for someone else.

8 · Reading the body

Describe, never judge. Pick a pattern.

Posture does not reliably predict pain

Many people with striking asymmetry have no pain, and many with textbook alignment do (Lederman's critique of the posture-pain model). Do not tell a Cultivator her posture is the cause of her symptoms, and do not promise that changing it will fix them. Posture is information about habit, nothing more. Established that the link is weak.

Ask permission before looking closely, offer to read your own posture first, and never comment on a body in front of a group.

Where the body holds

Tap a number on the body. Each area shows the muscle and fascia involved, a gentle self-massage, and a movement to try. Interpretive

An honest frame. There is no proof that fascia stores memories. These are the areas where many people report bracing, because they are the muscles of startle, guarding and holding the breath. Treat the map as a teaching tool. Go slowly, keep your choices, and stop at anything sharp or overwhelming.

Pick an area on the body.

Release practices (Waking the Tiger principles)

Peter Levine teaches that protective responses can be completed gently, in small steps. These practices are built on his ideas: orient, titrate, pendulate, complete. They are not exercises copied from the book and they do not replace therapy. Interpretive

If you feel flooded, numb or shaky in a way that frightens you, stop. Look around the room, feel your feet, drink water, and reach out to someone you trust or a therapist. You never have to go further than feels okay.

The wave rises, peaks and falls when it has room and small steps.

Before you begin, slide to where you are now (0 = shaky, 10 = very steady). After, slide again.

Poses and Practice Library

Schematic diagrams for the key practices, then the full library. Every practice has a dose, two tracks, a caution and a source. Nothing here asks you to push through.

The diagrams are simple on purpose. Follow the words, use props, and choose the track your body asks for. No pose in this course opens the legs wide or hangs the head back.

Back to Earth Wk 2

Lie on the back, a thin roll under the neck, a folded towel at the waist if the low back lifts, knees bent and together, toes turned slightly in. Rest 2 to 12 minutes.

Source: Avison step 1. Diagram is schematic and original to this course.

Foot tripod and inner arch lift Wk 4

Stand with soft knees. Feel behind the big toe, behind the little toe and the heel. Lift the inner arch gently without curling the toes.

Source: Segarra ch. 2, 5. Diagram is schematic and original to this course.

The Wave Wk 5

Feet press down, the pelvis rolls from the tailbone, the spine peels up one bone at a time, then comes down one bone at a time. No gripping of the buttocks.

Source: Avison step 5. Diagram is schematic and original to this course.

Chair rise Wk 5

Press the feet down, lift with the buttocks, stand tall, sit back slowly. Five rounds. Hand on a wall for any single-leg version.

Source: Segarra ch. 3. Diagram is schematic and original to this course.

Knee squeeze with block Wk 5

Block between the knees. Inhale. Press the feet down and, on the exhale, squeeze the knees gently toward each other. Legs stay together.

Source: Segarra ch. 5, 9. Diagram is schematic and original to this course.

Little Boat Wk 6

Hold the knees and rock a very small amount for 1 to 3 minutes. No grip.

Source: Avison step 3. Diagram is schematic and original to this course.

Cat-style pandiculation Wk 6

Squeeze gently along the back for three breaths, then let it stretch long as you do on waking, then rest. Three rounds.

Source: Schleip and Muller. Diagram is schematic and original to this course.

Standing Wave (Taranga) Wk 6

Soft knees, hands on thighs. Inhale: lift the breastbone. Exhale: draw the navel back and round. Five rounds.

Source: Segarra ch. 5. Diagram is schematic and original to this course.

Lying Spinal Twist Wk 7

Knees fall to one side onto a block, arms out, shoulders heavy. Five breaths, pause in the center, compare, then the other side. Never force.

Source: Avison step 4. Diagram is schematic and original to this course.

Seated side wiggle and crescent Wk 7

Sway slowly side to side and let the ribs fan open on the long side. Then a gentle crescent bend, only as far as is easy.

Source: Segarra ch. 3. Diagram is schematic and original to this course.

Palms up, palms down Wk 8

Arms out to the sides. Palms up opens the front of the chest; palms down engages the back of the shoulder blades. Three breaths each.

Source: Segarra ch. 6. Diagram is schematic and original to this course.
